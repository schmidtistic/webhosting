Home Office Built-In

A spare bedroom became an office, but the closet kept giving it away, and a large printer sat on the floor in everyone's way. Gutted the closet and built a floor-to-header built-in in its place: a base cabinet that hides the printer with permanent power inside it, curved verticals cut on a CNC, a back wall built twice, and 24V lighting routed into the shelf edges. It also spends all day as a video-call backdrop, which was the plan from the beginning.

Phase by Phase

Phase 01

A Closet Pretending to Be an Office

The room was a bedroom before it was an office, and the closet made sure everyone knew it. A wide opening, a rod, a shelf, and nothing about it that belonged in a room where work happens. The printer is the other half of the problem: it is large, it is not attractive, and it lived on the floor on a scrap of plywood with its cord running across the carpet to the nearest outlet. Gutting the closet was the easy decision. What replaced it had to do three things at once: stop reading as a closet, swallow the printer whole, and still be somewhere you would want to look.

The closet gutted, printer on the floor on scrap plywood, cord running across the carpet, February 2026
Phase 02

The Base Cabinet

Two carcasses built as a single run, sized so the deepest thing that had to disappear would fit with room to spare. Doors hung flat and full-overlay so the front would read as one surface rather than a row of boxes.

A word about the material, since it is the part most people would leave out. The visible faces are oak plywood, and where every side of a piece would show, it is solid oak, so nothing needed edge banding. Everywhere else, and especially once material started running short, the answer was cheaper plywood doubled up behind oak ply with banded edges. It looks like a solid oak build. It is not one. It is a frugal build wearing oak where oak gets seen, which is a decision with a long history in this family.

Phase 03

Power, and the Printer

The only power in the opening was the closet light switch, so the switch became the source for everything. Constant power off the line side runs to a receptacle inside the cabinet, which is where the printer plugs in and stays plugged in — no reaching, no unplugging, no cord leaving the box. The switched leg feeds a second outlet that the 24V lighting supplies plug into, so the wall switch that used to run a bare closet bulb now runs the entire built-in's lighting, and anything else added later can borrow from either side.

The test fit is the photo worth keeping. Printer in, doors open, and the furring strips already on the back wall for the paneling that comes next.

Printer test-fit inside the cabinet with the doors open, furring strips up on the back wall, April 2026
Phase 04

The Back Wall, Twice

The first back wall went up and the seams landed in open field, straight across the middle of the opening where there was nothing to break them up. From three feet away it looked like what it was: sheets of plywood butted together.

The fix came out of a second problem. Material was running short, so rather than buy more, the answer was to build a new ledge with a raised floor higher up the opening. That did two things at once: it cut down how much vertical back wall there was left to cover, and it let the remaining seams fall exactly where the vertical dividers would eventually stand. The seams are still there. They are just behind something now.

This is the part of the build worth remembering — the constraint produced the geometry that solved the flaw.

Phase 05

The Curve

The vertical dividers were originally going to run the full depth of the opening, straight out to the front edge. Mocked up that way, each bay turned into a deep, dark slot and the whole thing looked like storage instead of a built-in. The fix was to cut a soft stocking profile into each vertical so it sweeps back from the front edge and opens the bays up.

Every other cut on this project was made with a handheld router. This stock was too thick for that to end well, so a friend with a CNC router made the two cuts. It is the only part of the build with outside hands on it.

Phase 06

Lighting the Shelves

The shelves are lit from inside their own front edge. A channel is routed down the length of the shelf edge, a 24V LED strip is set into the channel, and a silicone diffuser cover presses in over the top. No visible strip, no row of dots, no aluminum extrusion stuck on afterward — just a continuous line of light sitting flush in the wood. Color and brightness run off a remote.

The piece in these photos never made it into the build. It was cut for a run across the bottom, and once it was in place it looked wrong and got pulled. The method is what survived, and it is the same method in every lit shelf on the wall.

Phase 07

Finished

Stained, lit, and loaded. The bays hold what bays hold. The center is deliberately open at desk height with a single framed piece behind it, because the whole wall was always going to be a video-call backdrop and that is the part the camera sees. It works better than expected — people on calls ask whether it is a virtual background.

Handles are not on yet. They are coming.

What's Left

The Last Five Percent

Finished enough to use every day. Not finished.

Handles

The doors are still bare fronts. Pulls are picked out in theory and not on the cabinet in practice. This is the honest last item.

Lighting Control

The shelf lighting runs off its own remote. Every other light in the house answers to Home Assistant. Bringing this one in line is the obvious next move.
